The taper vs. cold turkey

The clinical literature leans toward what you're already doing. A 2022 review in Addiction concludes that, as with other substances, "an incremental and slow reduction in cannabis intake and/or use of lower-THC products over an extended period (weeks) may reduce the probability and severity of withdrawal symptoms."1 That is the direct evidentiary basis for a stepped cap like this one.

The catch, stated honestly

There is no single validated taper schedule — no study prescribes "cut 25% every two days." The support for tapering is mechanistic and clinical-consensus rather than a proven protocol, so your 22 → 16 → 12 → 8 → 2–3 → 0 plan is a reasonable, literature-consistent structure, not a number handed down from a trial. That's fine; it's why the app holds a step for you when a day runs over.

Why reducing THC gradually works, biologically. Human lab studies show that oral THC (dronabinol) dose-dependently suppresses cannabis withdrawal — higher doses blunt the most symptoms, with few side effects.2 Chronic THC dosing likewise reduces withdrawal and self-administration in daily users.3 In plain terms: withdrawal is driven by the brain adjusting to less THC, so easing the drop gives your receptors time to catch up instead of hitting them with the full gap at once.

When a step is too big, three things can give

The app offers all three. First, it can push the date on its own — a day that runs over its cap holds that step an extra day. Those automatic days are deliberately finite (five for the whole descent), because reactive date-creep from a string of hard days is a different thing from a pace you chose. Second, you can take smaller cuts: the finer ladder splits each remaining step in two — from 8 the descent becomes 8 → 5 → 2–3 → 0 instead of 8 → 2–3 → 0 — and because a full step is two days where a half-step is one, zero does not move at all. The trade is drop size against settling time, not against the date. Third, you can deliberately take more time at each — the gentle path holds every remaining ceiling about a week instead of two days, which does move zero later, on purpose.

Which one fits depends on what the evidence actually says about pace. The same review frames the reduction as happening "over an extended period (weeks)"1, and the heavier and longer the use, the further toward the slow end it points. A ten-day descent is defensible but on the fast end for a decade of daily use; a three-to-four-week version — each step held about a week — is closer to what the literature describes, and that is exactly what the gentle path is. The finer ladder is the cheaper first reach when a single cut feels too steep (it spends nothing against the date); the gentle path is the one to choose when the whole pace feels too fast — which, for a long daily habit, the evidence supports. And note the asymmetry: smaller cuts don't buy time; only more days do.

The honest caveats, all the same size as the plan itself: cannabis withdrawal is uncomfortable but not medically dangerous, so pace is a question of comfort and relapse odds, not safety;1 no trial compares a six-hit drop against two three-hit drops, or ten days against thirty, in cannabis; and no study shows a taper beats stopping abruptly on success rate. None of the three is wrong — the finer ladder spends no date, the gentle path is where the pace evidence leans for heavy long-term use, and a hard day can always just hold.

And when the plan has already broken

All three of those soften steps that are still coming. They share an assumption: that the descent, softened, can be held from where it stands. Sometimes it can't — the extension days are spent and the last several days are still landing whole levels above the cap. A ceiling the days sail past isn't being missed so much as it has stopped operating, and at that point, and only then, the schedule card offers a fourth move: rebuild the descent. Re-enter the ladder at the ceiling your own log shows you can hold, then take the remaining steps as half-cuts with two days each. This one moves the date and does not pretend otherwise; what it buys is a plan that binds again. The reasoning comes from the lapse literature rather than the taper literature — a first miss predicts little, while accumulating misses predict progression — so the correction targets the pattern, not the individual day. It is offered once, chosen on a dated tap, with the arithmetic stated before you take it: a second rebuild would just be the drift the finite budget exists to refuse, and if the rebuilt descent breaks too, the honest next step is the relapse playbook and a professional look, not more schedule.

And when the descent held but the landing didn't

There is one failure the four moves above can't reach, because it isn't a failure of the descent at all. Every ladder in this plan ends the same way — the evening sitting, then nothing — which makes the last step the largest relative drop in the whole schedule: each rung above it cuts by a fraction, and the end asks for everything at once. A person can walk the entire staircase, hold 2–3 for days, and still find hits on the record the morning zero arrives. The schedule's normal rule is that a zero-day slip is the record's business, not the schedule's — the engine will not quietly move zero for it, ever. But when the quit never took hold at all — use on zero day or the day after, no clean run yet — the app makes its one dated exception and offers a fifth move: rebuild the landing. The last stair splits instead of being jumped: re-enter at the sitting the log shows holding, then evenings smaller than the plan ever wrote — 2, then 1, two days each — and the same zero at the bottom, about six days out. Three things are stated on the card before you take it, and they bear repeating here. A softer landing eases withdrawal; it does not improve the odds of staying stopped — those get made after zero either way. The move exists once — a landing that can be rebuilt forever is zero receding one mercy at a time. And it is only offered while the stash ritual hasn't sealed the road: once what's left is in the trash, this app will not write a schedule that means going out to buy more. If zero held for even a couple of days before the slip, the answer isn't this — it's the relapse playbook, where a lapse costs a run and never the physiology.

The playbook, day by day

The taper shrinks three things at once: the daily cap (22 → 16 → 12 → 8 → 2–3 → 0), the size of a sitting (4 → 3 → 2–3 hits), and — most of all — the number of windows in a day (6 → 4 → 3 → 2 → 1 → 0). That last one is the real engine: every step deletes a window or pushes the survivors further apart, so the decline is built into the clock, not into willpower.

Halved steps run the same three shrinkages through twice as many levels — 22 → 19 → 16 → 14 → 12 → 10 → 8 → 5 → 2–3 → 0, a day each instead of two — so the windows come off one at a time rather than in pairs. The gentle path instead keeps the standard ceilings and holds each about a week, stretching the same descent over three to four weeks. Each day below is written for the standard schedule; on the other two the app carries its own copy for every step, and the day card is always the authority over this page.

If a day runs over, the plan doesn’t break — that step holds one extra day at its widest gap, and every later step (zero day included) slides back with it. A held day repeats its last day’s plan; it never loosens. And a genuinely rough patch slows the whole descent: every two hard days at one cap ease the following step by an extra day, because the drop after a struggling step is the risky one. The windows above are one clean way to lay each day out, not a law — what’s fixed is the count of windows, the sitting size, and the gap. And if zero eve arrives before you feel ready, the app offers one planned extra evening-only day from the ritual card — a planned day beats an unplanned slip.

Cannabis withdrawal — the real timeline

Cannabis withdrawal is real, formally recognized (DSM-5), and — for a ten-year daily user — expected. A meta-analysis pooling >20,000 regular and dependent users found roughly 47% experience clinically meaningful withdrawal on standardized scales.1 Heavier, longer use sits at the higher end of that risk. None of it is medically dangerous in an otherwise-healthy adult — the worst of it reads like a rough flu week.

Abrupt stops from heavy use — the worst case, not your forecast.

Read this timeline with the taper in mind. The figures below come overwhelmingly from users stopping abruptly from heavy use. The same review's rationale for tapering is that a slow reduction "may reduce the probability and severity of withdrawal symptoms,"1 and the THC-dosing trials show symptom load tracks the size of the drop.2,3 Arriving at zero from 2–3 hits a day is a far smaller drop than from ~30 — expect the blunted version of everything below. The timeline is the map of the worst case, not a forecast.

When it hits, and for how long

  • First 24–48 hours: symptoms begin.1 Expect the first ripples on days 1–2 after whatever zero date the app currently shows — held days slide it later.
  • Days 2–6: the peak. Most symptoms are strongest here1 — the "why am I doing this" window. Lab data agrees peak effects land around days 2–4.2 Clear this and the grade flattens.
  • Weeks 2–3: the climb back. In heavy users symptoms can run 2–3 weeks or longer.1 Ten years daily — give it the full three weeks.
  • Sleep is the long tail. Sleep disturbance (and vivid dreams — REM rebound after years of suppression) may persist for several weeks or more,1 outlasting everything else. A bad night at week four means nothing.

The symptoms themselves

The common ones: irritability, anxiety/nervousness, sleep trouble and strange dreams, reduced appetite, restlessness, depressed mood, and cravings — plus sometimes physical notes like headache, sweating, or stomach upset. This is the exact cluster the taper and the app's 20-minute delay are built to shave down. The dronabinol trials specifically show the biggest levers are mood, irritability, sleep difficulty, and craving,3 so those are what to watch and support.

One boundary worth marking: withdrawal ends. If low mood, anxiety, or broken sleep are still running steady past week four — a pattern, not the odd bad night — that's no longer withdrawal. Cannabis is often quietly medicating something underneath, and zero is when that something surfaces. A professional conversation at that point is following the evidence, not failing it.

Because the peak is days 2–6, front-load your support there: clear your calendar where you can, line up food and sleep aids before zero, and treat that first week as the project — not the weeks after.

What actually helps

Behavioral — the first line

The review is blunt: no medication is approved for cannabis withdrawal, so supportive counselling and psychoeducation (knowing what's coming — which is the point of this page) are the first-line approach.1 The two most effective stand-alone psychological treatments are cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) and motivational enhancement therapy (MET), and they work best combined; contingency management (concrete rewards for clean time) is an effective add-on.1

  • Delay every urge. Cravings fire on old cue-times and pass in minutes — the app's 20-minute timer is this, operationalized.
  • Reward the streak. Give yourself something real for each day held. That's contingency management in miniature.
  • Guard sleep deliberately — but with the parts that work. When CBT-I is broken into components and tested, sleep restriction and stimulus control carry the effect; sleep hygiene (the wind-down, the dark room) does not. Hold a fixed wake time, keep the bed for sleep only, and go to bed only when sleepy. Sleep is the symptom most often named in going back.
  • Move. Exercise is a low-risk, broadly-recommended aid for mood and sleep during withdrawal.

Medication — proceed only with a clinician

Nothing here is a prescription; it's what the evidence shows, to discuss with a doctor. Trials of gabapentin and nabiximols (a THC/CBD spray) have reduced withdrawal or use, but in small, largely unreplicated studies — promising, not settled.1 N-acetylcysteine (NAC) is popular and low-risk but has mixed evidence, especially in adults. Over-the-counter sleep aids get used short-term for the sleep tail. Bottom line: none is proven enough to self-medicate a 10-day taper — behavioral tools are the backbone.

You're doing this self-directed and healthy, which is reasonable for cannabis. But if withdrawal becomes unmanageable, or there's heavy alcohol or other drug use in the mix, that changes the risk picture — loop in a clinician rather than pushing through alone.

Staying quit

Quitting cannabis after years of daily use is genuinely hard and lapses are common — that's the norm, not a personal failure. What the evidence and the app's philosophy share: the trend line matters, not any single day.

  • A slip is data, not doom. During the taper the app holds that cap an extra day by itself and keeps stepping down. After zero there is no cap to hold: the slip is recorded, the run restarts the next day, and your clean total and best run are never taken back.
  • Strip the environment. The night before zero, clear the flower, pieces, and papers, so home isn't a loaded cue during the days 2–6 peak.
  • Tell someone. Social support and knowing the timeline are protective — a briefed friend who won't enable a supply run is worth a lot in week one.
  • Watch the trade. A nightcap or a vape quietly taking over the old evening slot is a swap, not an exit — alcohol especially, which worsens the exact sleep rebound it gets recruited to fix. Give the old windows new rituals, not new substances.
  • Expect the sleep tail and don't over-read it. Weeks of odd sleep and vivid dreams are the recovery, not a relapse warning.

After zero — the twelve weeks that matter

The taper is the part that gets the attention, and on the evidence it is the part that matters least. Tapering has support for easing withdrawal, not for improving the odds of stopping. A Cochrane review of 22 studies and 9,219 people found reduce-to-quit and abrupt quitting produce equivalent long-term quit rates (RR 1.01, 95% CI 0.87–1.17, moderate certainty), and the largest single trial found abrupt cessation did better — about 25% more likely to be abstinent at four weeks and six months, even among people who said they would rather taper. There is no cannabis taper RCT at all; this is borrowed from tobacco. So the schedule here is a reasonable structure resting on thin ice, and the app now says so on the Plan tab.

The risk runs after zero, not before it. In outpatient samples roughly a quarter lapse within a month, near half within three months, and about seven in ten within six. That is why the app opens a twelve-week structure at zero instead of handing over a day counter.

Sleep — the long tail

Poor sleep after a quit attempt is a documented lapse risk; around 65% of people name it as contributing, and among those with poor sleep in a previous abstinence attempt, 48–77% reported relapsing or substituting another substance to manage it. So sleep is worth guarding — with the parts that actually work.

When CBT-I is decomposed and its components tested against each other, sleep restriction and stimulus control carry the effect; sleep hygiene does not. Sleep hygiene — the wind-down, the dark room — is what almost every app ships. Fully automated digital CBT-I does work — meta-analyses covering 73 trials and over 14,000 participants show real reductions in insomnia severity, with roughly 250 minutes of protocol the sweet spot.

The two that carry the effect are simple to apply yourself: keep the wake time fixed no matter how the night went, and use the bed only for sleep — go when you're sleepy, and if you're lying awake, get up and come back when you are. Restricting time in bed toward the sleep that is actually happening builds sleep pressure and works faster, but expect to feel sleepier before better; don't drive drowsy, and talk to a doctor first if you have bipolar disorder, a seizure disorder, a parasomnia, or untreated sleep apnea. Following Spielman's protocol, never compress the window below about 5½ hours.

On sleeping pills: in a randomised trial of extended-release zolpidem for cannabis use disorder, it did settle sleep — and the sleep problems returned when the drug stopped, with no significant gain in abstinence. Worth knowing before treating a prescription as the answer.

If–then plans

Forming an implementation intention — "if specific cue, then specific response" — has meta-analytic support for reducing substance use at small but real effect sizes (around d = .31 for drinking and smoking), and costs nothing. The effect depends on the cue being concrete, which is why the app mines your own logged timestamps for the hours you actually reach, rather than offering generic advice.

The ladder

Contingency management is the best-evidenced treatment for cannabis use disorder. The trial schedule escalates with each consecutive clean sample and resets on a lapse — $2.50, then $3.75, then $5.00 and upward, running toward roughly $1,000 across twelve weeks. The app keeps that shape and scales the magnitude, because the trials paid per urine sample three times a week and the app pays daily.

One honest difference: those trials verified abstinence independently. A ladder you pay yourself on your own word is the weak form of it. Naming someone who holds the money and asks is the closest an app gets to the thing that was actually tested.

Why the schedule stops extending

A first lapse predicts remarkably little. In a study tracking 203 smokers across 1,001 lapse episodes in real time, responses to the first lapse — self-blame, guilt, falling confidence — predicted nothing. Responses to each additional lapse did, with drops in self-efficacy accelerating progression to the next one. An engine that silently absorbs the sixth missed cap exactly as it absorbed the first is manufacturing that pattern. So extension is now a finite budget: five days for the whole descent, shown depleting, and when it is spent the schedule stops moving zero and the choice becomes explicit.
